\section{The question}

Lenders are deploying models into two decisions that shape household wealth:
\textbf{consumer installment credit} and \textbf{residential mortgage
origination}. Both are regulated --- ECOA and Regulation B govern both, the Fair
Housing Act adds to mortgage. The question is not whether bias matters here but
what a team must actually \emph{do} to keep it out, and whether the standard
toolchain gets them there.

We built a full audit for both sectors on real data (1{,}000 consumer credit and
10{,}978 Georgia mortgage applications) and measured what it took to make that
audit correct. Most of what we learned came from the ways it was wrong first.

\section{What the standard toolchain does and does not give you}

We checked AIF360 0.6 and Fairlearn 0.13 directly rather than assuming.

\textbf{Which outcome is ``favorable''?} AIF360 defaults to
\texttt{favorable\_label=1.0}. On a target named \texttt{loan\_default} that
means \emph{defaulting is the good outcome}. We reproduce it: the default returns
$\mathrm{DI}=1.2072$ where the correct orientation returns $0.9862$. Fairlearn has
no favorable-label parameter at all. Credit-risk targets are almost always
adverse-coded, so the default is almost always wrong.

\textbf{How much data per subgroup?} Neither toolkit has \emph{any} concept of
minimum subgroup size --- no floor, no warning, no guidance. Every team invents
its own.

\textbf{Who counts as protected?} Neither binds a protected class to a statute.
You pass a column; nothing checks it against ECOA. Nor does either record which
reference group produced a ratio.

\medskip\noindent The toolkits compute correctly. They do not stop you answering
four regulatory questions by accident.

\section{What we got wrong, and what it cost}

Because nothing supplied them, we invented our own floor ($n \geq 15$), severity
bands, and output checks. Each changed a conclusion.

\textbf{Sample size inverted a disparity.} Auditing a 2{,}196-row sample, our
mortgage audit ranked \textbf{American Indian men as the best-treated group}
($\mathrm{DI}=1.1504$) --- on four applicants who all happened to be approved.
Over all 10{,}978 they are the \textbf{worst} group in the data ($0.6734$). The
floor was not the error: the cell it suppressed, at $0.1917$, was itself noise
($0.8815$ at full population). At small $n$ the estimates are wrong in
\emph{both} directions, and no floor can tell the cases apart.

\textbf{We audited age under the wrong statute.} We used $40{+}$ --- that is the
ADEA, employment law. Credit is ECOA, and Regulation B \S1002.2(o) protects
$62{+}$. On our threshold the model looked clean ($0.9791$); on the statutory one
it shows the largest age disparity in the data ($\mathbf{0.8897}$). The flag we
needed was in the raw HMDA file, unused.

\textbf{Our automated checks punished correct answers.} A 40-character minimum
marked 6 correct answers as refusals, then zeroed 14 evaluations of a second AI
provider ($70.0 \rightarrow 19.79$) --- on which we blocked that vendor. A
fabrication check flagged a newer model $5\times$ more often, but $67\%$ of those
flags were arithmetic it was asked to do ($88.88\%$ is $0.8888$).

\section{What it takes: a protocol for these two sectors}

\textbf{1.\ State the favorable outcome explicitly}, every time; never accept the
default. \textbf{2.\ Audit the book, not a test split} --- in mortgage, HMDA
already requires you to file it. \textbf{3.\ Bind the protected class to the
governing statute in code}, citation beside the threshold: ECOA $62{+}$, not ADEA
$40{+}$. Note the asymmetry a parity metric cannot express --- \S1002.6(b)(2)
permits \emph{favouring} elderly applicants, so a ratio above 1 is lawful.
\textbf{4.\ Publish what you suppressed}, with $n$ and the unreported ratio; a
silent floor is indistinguishable from a clean result. \textbf{5.\ Record the
reference group} beside every ratio --- most-favoured-cell versus designated
control moves verdicts across the four-fifths line. \textbf{6.\ Test every
automated gate where the right answer is unusual}: the terse answer, the small
subgroup, the model that shows its work. All three of ours failed exactly there.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Why it matters.} Every failure above produced a
\emph{clean} result --- the dangerous direction in lending. A false alarm costs a
reviewer an afternoon; a false clearance closes the question and the applicant
never learns why. An audit that cannot say what sample it ran on and what it
withheld cannot support the clearance it is used to give.
