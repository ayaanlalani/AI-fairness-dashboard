\section{Why we did this}

We set out to answer a practical question: \emph{can a language model be trusted
to interpret fairness metrics?} Companies are already wiring LLMs into compliance
tooling, and nobody had built ground truth that wasn't itself a model's opinion.
So we built it --- a deterministic pipeline that computes every fairness number
and assigns every severity label by fixed rule, with the model allowed to
interpret and nothing else.

Then we noticed we were validating a model against an audit pipeline that nobody
had validated. So we audited the audit. That is this paper.

\section{The result that reframed the project}

Our mortgage audit ranked \textbf{American Indian men as the \emph{best}-treated
group} --- disparate impact $1.15$, above parity, nothing to see. It was looking
at four of them. All four happened to be approved.

Auditing all $10{,}978$ applicants instead of a $2{,}196$-row sample, the same
model on the same data puts that group at $\mathbf{0.6734}$: \textbf{the worst
group in the dataset}, in the \textsc{critical} band, $31$ people.

The audit did not merely miss a disparity. It \textbf{inverted} it, and reported
the inversion with a clean severity label. Nothing was broken --- the metrics
were right, the code was right, the reporting floor that hid the small groups was
statistically correct to hide them. The sample was simply too small for the
affected groups to be visible at all, and nothing in the output said so.

\section{Why this matters}

\textbf{Audits are becoming gates.} Bias-audit requirements are entering
regulation, procurement, and model sign-off. Increasingly a system ships because
an audit says so. Every failure above produced a \emph{clean} result --- the
direction nobody double-checks. A false alarm costs a reviewer an afternoon; a
false clearance closes the question permanently.

\textbf{Nobody audits the auditor.} These were not bugs and nobody was careless.
They are defaults --- chosen once, applied everywhere, visible only in source
code. When an audit was a human report, its assumptions were arguable. When an
audit is a pipeline, its assumptions are invisible.

\textbf{This is not just fairness.} Our headline model score was $75\%$; it
decomposes to $33\%/92\%/100\%$, where the $100\%$ is the case so easy that
agreeing costs nothing. Any benchmark averaging over uneven tasks can report
difficulty as if it were capability.

\section{How to use this}

\textbf{If you run audits:} audit your whole population, not a convenience
sample. Print what your size floor \emph{withheld}, with $n$ --- ours printed only
what passed. Put the statute citation next to the threshold in code. State the
favorable outcome explicitly; never infer it from label values.

\textbf{If you build audit tooling:} treat every mechanical gate as a hypothesis
and test it on the cases where the right answer is unusual --- the terse answer,
the six-person subgroup, the model that shows its work. All three of ours failed
exactly there.

\textbf{If you buy or regulate audits:} ask what sample the audit ran on and what
it suppressed. An audit that cannot answer those two questions cannot support the
clearance it is being used to give.

\textbf{If you want to reuse it:} the pipeline, the frozen prompts and every
number here are released and regenerate offline, no API key, for \$0.89 of
compute.
