Deterministic Ground Truth for Auditing LLM Fairness Interpretation}

\begin{abstract}
Large language models are increasingly proposed as fairness auditors, but
evaluating them requires ground truth that does not itself come from a model.
We release a benchmark over three lending use cases --- consumer credit (German
Credit), mortgage origination (HMDA Georgia), and peer-to-peer default risk
(Lending Club) --- in which a deterministic AIF360 pipeline computes every
metric and assigns every severity label, and the LLM is scored only on its
interpretation of that fixed context. The benchmark ships 36 frozen prompts, a
no-API-key replay path, two anti-hallucination detectors, and an enforced
cumulative cost cap; full reproduction costs \$0.71. Our results argue against
the summary statistic our own benchmark produces. Aggregate severity agreement
for \texttt{gpt-4o} is 75\%, but it decomposes to 33\%, 92\%, and 100\% across
the three use cases, and that ordering tracks how discriminating each underlying
classifier is rather than model capability. Three of nine attribute pairs
changed severity label across four prompt strategies on identical input.
Embedding retrieved literature in the prompt cut fabricated citations from 16 to
2 like-for-like. We also report two findings against our own instruments: a
refusal detector that flagged six correct terse answers as refusals, and a
guardrail that penalised the epistemically correct response to an $n{=}6$
subgroup. Separately, a metric-orientation error --- computing selection rates
on the unfavorable label --- produced a disparate-impact ratio of $0.0$ where
the correct value is $0.9931$, from a single convention applied to a target
named \texttt{loan\_default}. Following \citet{lee2020}, we pair every
statistical finding with its decision-context interpretation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A fairness audit is a claim about a decision that affects people. A disparate
impact ratio of $0.86$ is not a finding on its own; it becomes one when attached
to who was denied credit, what recourse they had, and what the institution is
obliged to check. \citet{lee2020} make this the organising principle of
human-centered fair AI: fairness is defined relative to a human decision
context, not in metric space. We take that framing seriously as a constraint on
what a benchmark can claim.

Recent work has sharpened the practical version of this problem.
\citet{zaccour2025} show that quantitative algorithm audits are frequently
blocked by data access rather than by method, and \citet{stumpf2024} argue that
fairness and correctness assessments need user-centred evaluation rather than
metric reporting alone. \citet{kim2025} survey how equity is operationalised
across HCI and fairness research and find the construct fragmenting under the
practical demands placed on it. \citet{mei2025} demonstrate, in a speech
setting, that audit \emph{pipelines} themselves introduce pitfalls that change
conclusions. Our contribution sits in that last category: we build the audit
infrastructure, then report where the infrastructure misleads.

LLMs are an attractive candidate for the interpretive layer, because generating
decision-context narrative from computed metrics is exactly what they are good
at. But evaluating them needs ground truth that is not another model's output.
This benchmark supplies that by construction: Python computes the metrics and
assigns severity via a fixed rule; the model never recomputes a number, and
asserting an unprovided value is recorded as a hallucination, not a result.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item A three-use-case lending benchmark with deterministic severity ground
        truth, spanning protected classes and economic proxies, and spanning a
        discriminating and a near-degenerate classifier.
  \item 36 released frozen prompts with a \texttt{--dry-run} path that
        reproduces the entire scoring pipeline with no API key, plus two
        anti-hallucination detectors and an enforced cumulative cost cap.
  \item Two-track evaluation that keeps distinct questions distinct: whether the
        model interprets metrics reliably (Track Q), and whether it raises the
        audit-quality ceiling (Track H), never scored against each other.
  \item Negative and self-critical results: an aggregate agreement number we
        argue should not be quoted, and two cases where our own measuring
        instruments penalise the better answer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Benchmark design}

\subsection{Use cases}

We build on German Credit \citep{germancredit} and HMDA Georgia
\citep{hmda}, plus a Lending Club peer-to-peer default task. The three are
deliberately not three instances of one setting (Table~\ref{tab:usecases}).
They differ in the two properties that most change what an audit can conclude:
whether the protected attribute is a protected class or an economic proxy, and
whether the classifier discriminates enough for disparity to be measurable at
all. Lending discrimination is well documented empirically across exactly this
span --- appearance and gender effects in peer-to-peer markets
\citep{duarte2012,pope2011}, FinTech-era consumer lending
\citep{bartlett2019}, local credit-market conditions \citep{butler2017}, and
historical records used as training data \citep{sarwal2024} --- which is what
makes the lifecycle framing tractable rather than arbitrary.

\subsection{Deterministic layer}

For each protected attribute we compute disparate impact (DI), demographic
parity difference, equal-opportunity difference (EOD), average odds difference
(AOD), and the Theil index using AIF360 \citep{aif360}, with Fairlearn
\citep{fairlearn} mitigations as the reference vocabulary; the canonical
interventions the guardrail expects a remediation to name are the standard
pre- and post-processing family \citep{kamiran2011,feldman2015}. A rule-based
\texttt{classify\_severity} maps DI to \textsc{low}/\textsc{moderate}/%
\textsc{high}/\textsc{critical}. This function, not a model, is the ground
truth. Intersectional cells are reported with $n$ and suppressed below
$n \geq 15$.

Two conventions are pinned by unit tests because both silently changed results
during development: the favorable-label orientation (\S\ref{sec:orientation})
and the sign of AOD, whose two compute paths disagreed on
privileged-minus-unprivileged ordering while severity narratives quote the sign.

\subsection{Frozen prompts and the two tracks}

Each (use case $\times$ attribute) pair is rendered into four prompt
strategies --- \texttt{zero\_shot}, \texttt{chain\_of\_thought},
\texttt{self\_critique}, \texttt{constrained} --- giving
$3 \times 3 \times 4 = 36$ prompts. Every prompt
embeds the computed metric context, the use-case framing, and retrieved
Semantic Scholar evidence, and is stored in full alongside its scored mock
response. The benchmark is therefore replayable without re-deriving context, and
the released packs double as a no-key regression test.

\textbf{Track Q} asks whether the model is a reliable metric interpreter, and is
scored against \texttt{classify\_severity}. \textbf{Track H} asks whether the
model raises the audit-quality ceiling, and is judged \emph{only} against an
H1--H5 checklist, a mechanical guardrail baseline, and a quality rubric ---
never against the deterministic numbers, since doing so would re-run Track Q
and would
penalise an audit for being more careful than a threshold table.

\subsection{Anti-hallucination and cost control}

Two detectors run on every response. The citation detector flags any reference
whose 3-grams do not appear in the retrieved titles; the metric detector flags
any asserted numeric value absent from the provided context, excluding canonical
thresholds. Both are unit-tested, including a deliberately hallucinated
$\mathrm{DI}=0.4321$. Spend is capped cumulatively across invocations with a
padded pre-call estimate, so the cap aborts before an overrun rather than after;
a persisted ledger makes the cap hold across the separate per-dataset runs.

\section{Results}

\subsection{UC1 German Credit: the marginal view hides the finding}

All three marginal DI values clear the four-fifths screen (sex $0.8595$, age
$0.8212$, foreign worker $0.9402$). The intersection does not:
\textbf{\texttt{under\_40}\,$\times$\,\texttt{female} has DI $0.6087$}
($n{=}46$), below the $0.72$ \textsc{critical} threshold that \emph{neither
marginal attribute approaches}. Auditing sex and age separately returns three
passes and misses the disadvantaged group.

The foreign-worker result is not a finding. Its privileged group has
\textbf{six records}, under the reporting floor, and the marginal is dominated by
the $n{=}194$ side. \emph{Decision-context reading:} for a loan officer, "this
model passes on sex and age" and "this model denies young women at $0.61$ the
rate of older women" are different operational facts, and only the second is
actionable.

\subsection{UC2 HMDA: same lesson, different mechanism}

\texttt{Black}\,$\times$\,\texttt{Female} ($n{=}410$) reaches DI $0.7821$
against \texttt{Asian}\,$\times$\,\texttt{Male} ($n{=}130$), exceeding both
marginals; both cells are adequately sized. The error profile is diagnostic:
race DI $0.8956$ fails the screen while $\mathrm{EOD}=0.0025$ and
$\mathrm{AOD}=0.0117$ are approximately zero. The model is \emph{not} making
differentially worse errors by race --- it is reproducing a base-rate difference
already present in the labels.

\emph{Decision-context reading:} this distinction determines who owns the fix.
Differential error is a modelling problem; base-rate bias is a policy and data
problem upstream of the model, and an equalised-odds post-processor would target
a defect this model does not have.

\subsection{UC3 Lending Club: a corrected artifact and an honest null}
\label{sec:orientation}

The target is \texttt{loan\_default}, so label $1$ is the \emph{unfavorable}
outcome. Computing selection rates on predicted default gave male $0.0000$
versus female $0.0069$, hence $\mathrm{DI}=0/0.0069=0.0$, reported as
\textsc{critical}. Oriented on the favorable outcome (predicted non-default),
the rates are $1.0000$ and $0.9931$ and \textbf{$\mathrm{DI}=0.9931$} --- near
parity. One convention moved the headline metric from maximally alarming to
unremarkable.

We stress that nothing here was exotic: a standard toolkit, clean data, a
correct metric implementation, and a target variable named for the adverse
event. \textbf{A practitioner doing everything else right can obtain a maximally
wrong conclusion from the orientation default alone.}

The near-parity values are not evidence of fairness. The classifier predicts
non-default for ${\sim}99.5\%$ of the test set (4 default predictions in 600), so
every group looks equal because the model barely discriminates. The honest
finding is \emph{insufficient model discrimination to measure disparity}. UC3
also supplies the benchmark's only economic-proxy contrast: near-parity on
\texttt{income\_level} means something different from near-parity on
\texttt{gender}, and the pipeline cannot tell them apart.

Aggregate severity agreement is $27/36$ ($75.0\%$). \textbf{We argue against
quoting that number.} It decomposes to german\_credit $33.3\%$, hmda $91.7\%$,
lending\_club $100\%$, and the ordering is not a capability ranking.
lending\_club's perfect score is the easiest possible case --- a near-degenerate
classifier makes every attribute a trivially correct near-parity \textsc{low}.
german\_credit is hardest: three \textsc{moderate} baselines near threshold
boundaries where small interpretive differences flip the label. Aggregate
agreement is thus a function of how discriminating the audited classifiers are,
and a single agreement number for an LLM fairness auditor is not a well-defined
quantity.

Errors are asymmetric in the safe direction: 7 over-escalations against 2
under-escalations. Under-escalation is the consequential failure for an audit
tool; over-escalation costs review time, not a missed finding.

\textbf{Three of nine attribute pairs changed severity label across the four
strategies on identical input context} (Figure~\ref{fig:matrix}). Same model,
same numbers, different phrasing, different verdict. This --- not the agreement
rate --- is the core reliability result, and it is why we do not recommend
letting a model \emph{set} a severity label.

\paragraph{Grounding suppresses fabrication.}
Comparing like-for-like on the two use cases also covered by an earlier
ungrounded run --- same model, same attributes, differing only in whether
retrieved literature was embedded --- fabricated citations fall from
\textbf{16 to 2} and mean score rises from $69.08$ to $76.67$
(Figure~\ref{fig:grounding}). Retrieval grounding is doing more for factuality
here than prompt engineering is.

\paragraph{Provider capability dominates.}
On the identical harness, Gemini 2.5 Flash refused 19 of 24 cycles with mean
score $19.79$. A benchmark of this shape measures provider capability at least
as much as method, which bounds how much any single-model result should be
generalised.

\subsection{Track H: audit quality}

Three narrative audits (o3, three self-refinement cycles each) passed $13/15$
mechanical guardrail gates and $13/15$ H1--H5 criteria, with \textbf{27/27
citations verified} against the retrieved evidence pool. A cross-model rubric
judge (\texttt{gpt-4o} grading o3) scored $4.8$, $4.7$, and $5.0$ out of $5$; we
report this as secondary evidence, since an LLM grading an LLM is weaker than a
mechanical gate.

Qualitatively, the narrative form supplied things the deterministic template
cannot. On foreign worker it reproduced the $n{=}6$ caveat and quantified it:
\emph{"one label change would swing DI to 0.78 (high severity). Thus,
statistical power is inadequate."} On UC3 it identified the degenerate
classifier unprompted and converted the null into a conditional prediction with
a mechanism and a citation. Both are decision-relevant statements with no field
in the template to hold them.

The contrast, not a score, is the Track H result: \textbf{the deterministic
pipeline establishes what is true; the narrative audit establishes what it means
for a decision.} They are not competing implementations, and "which is more
accurate" is a category error.

\subsection{Two findings against our own instruments}
\label{sec:instruments}

\paragraph{The refusal detector conflates brevity with refusal.}
It flagged 6 of 9 \texttt{constrained} responses; \textbf{none were genuine
refusals}. Any narrative field under 40 characters counts as effectively empty,
so \texttt{how\_to\_fix: "DisparateImpactRemover"} --- precise and on-spec in 22
characters --- registers as a refusal, under a prompt that asked for terse
output. Reporting a $66.7\%$ refusal rate would have attributed a measurement
artifact to the model. We left the detector unchanged, since it is part of the
frozen harness and altering it would invalidate the ungrounded comparison, and
report the distinction instead.

\paragraph{A guardrail penalised the correct answer.}
Our baseline requires every mitigation to name a canonical algorithm. The two
attributes that fail are exactly the two whose problem \emph{is} data scarcity
--- foreign worker ($n{=}6$) and loan-amount tier ($3.2\times$ imbalance) ---
where the audit instead proposed acquiring records to a stated floor, Bayesian
hierarchical shrinkage, bootstrapped confidence intervals, and manual review.
\textbf{No post-processor repairs an $n{=}6$ subgroup}; applying one would
manufacture false confidence. The guardrail encodes an assumption that every
fairness problem has an algorithmic fix.

Both were written in good faith by people who knew the domain, and both quietly
punish the better answer. This is the concrete form \citet{mei2025}'s
auditing-pitfalls argument takes in our setting: audit infrastructure carries
normative commitments, and they surface as penalties.

\section{Discussion}

Read through the \citet{lee2020} lens, our results are less about model
capability than about where a model can be trusted in a human decision pipeline.
The defensible division of labour is the one the benchmark enforces:
\emph{deterministic code owns the numbers and the thresholds; the model owns the
interpretation; and the interpretation is gated on evidence the model did not
invent.} Track Q supports the first clause --- severity labels move under
paraphrase --- and the 16-to-2 fabrication result supports the third.

Two implications for practice. First, marginal-only auditing, which is the
common case, systematically misses intersectional harm: in UC1 every marginal
passes while the joint group sits at $0.6087$. Second, threshold-as-gate reading
destroys information. UC2's race result means label bias, not model error; UC3's
null means the model is not deciding anything yet. Both are invisible if DI is
read as pass/fail --- which is how it tends to be used, and what
\citet{stumpf2024} and \citet{kim2025} identify as the gap between fairness
metrics and the assessments practitioners actually need.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Scale.} Three datasets, one primary model. This is a benchmark
        design with a worked demonstration, not a large-scale evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Subgroup sizes.} Foreign-worker privileged $n{=}6$; several HMDA
        race categories are severely sparse. Cells below $n \geq 15$ are
        suppressed and $n$ is always reported.
  \item \textbf{UC3 informativeness.} A classifier predicting one class
        ${\sim}99.5\%$ of the time cannot exhibit measurable disparity; UC3
        exercises the pipeline, not the fairness of a working model.
  \item \textbf{LLM nondeterminism.} No seed or temperature pinning. The
        four-cycle design varies prompt strategy, so cycle-to-cycle differences
        confound strategy with sampling noise; we cannot separate them.
  \item \textbf{API versioning.} \texttt{gpt-4o} and o3 are moving targets. The
        frozen packs make inputs reproducible; outputs are not.
  \item \textbf{Metric grounding.} All metrics are associational. Root-cause
        mapping is rule-based inference from metric values and feature
        correlations, \textbf{not causal identification}, and we make no causal
        claim.
  \item \textbf{No legal claim.} ECOA, Regulation B, and the four-fifths rule
        appear as decision context. A DI below $0.8$ is a screening signal, not a
        finding of unlawful discrimination.
  \item \textbf{Cost figures.} o3 is priced at the higher historical rate, so its
        cost is an upper bound rather than a billed amount.
  \item \textbf{Generality.} HMDA is Georgia only; German Credit is 1990s data
        used as a benchmark, not a current population.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

We release a lending-lifecycle fairness benchmark whose ground truth is
deterministic, whose 36 prompts are frozen and replayable without an API key,
and whose full reproduction costs under a dollar. Its most useful outputs are
the uncomfortable ones: an aggregate agreement statistic that we show is not a
well-defined quantity, severity labels that move under paraphrase, a
disparate-impact ratio that read $0.0$ instead of $0.9931$ because of one
orientation default, and two of our own measuring instruments penalising the
correct answer. Following \citet{lee2020}, we take the last group most
seriously: audit infrastructure is not a neutral lens, and a benchmark that
cannot detect its own biases is not measuring what it claims to.
